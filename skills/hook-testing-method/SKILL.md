# Testing hooks against your own audience

Every piece of hook advice in circulation is an average taken across audiences that are not yours.
"Start with a question." "Use a number." "Open with a contradiction." Each of those is true
somewhere and false somewhere else, and neither the advice nor the person repeating it knows which
one you have.

The fix is not better advice. It is a small amount of measurement, done properly, on your own
account. This skill explains how — by hand, with no tools required.

## What a hook is, and why it is the thing worth testing

The hook is the first line of a caption or the first second or two of a video: the part that decides
whether someone keeps going. On short-form video it is the highest-leverage thing you control,
because the platform decides how far to push a post largely on whether early viewers stayed.

It is worth testing *specifically* because it is short, cheap to vary, and its effect is large. Body
copy and hashtags matter far less, and testing them costs the same effort.

## The method

### 1. Hold the idea constant, vary only the opening

Pick one thing you want to say. Write three to five different openings for it. Everything else —
the point, the footage, the call to action — stays identical.

If you change the topic and the hook at once, a result tells you nothing: you cannot tell which
change moved the number. This is the single most common way informal testing wastes a month.

### 2. Publish them as a set, not as they occur to you

Post the variants under conditions as similar as you can manage — same account, similar times of
day, over a compact window. Not one this week and one next month, when your follower count,
the season, and the platform's behaviour have all moved.

You are not running a laboratory. You are trying to remove the differences you *can* remove, so the
opening is the biggest remaining one.

### 3. Measure retention, not applause

Rank variants on how far viewers got — watch time, average view duration, retention at three
seconds, whatever your platform exposes. Not likes, not comments, not follows.

Likes measure whether people who already finished approved. The hook's job is upstream of that: it
decides who finished at all. A variant can win on retention and lose on likes, and the retention
result is the one that tells you what to write next time.

### 4. Wait for enough posts before believing anything

This is where almost everyone goes wrong, and it is worth being blunt: **one post is not a result.**

Short-form reach is extremely skewed. A single video can get ten times its neighbour's views because
of who happened to see it first, and no property of the writing explains it. If you call a winner
after one post per variant, you are reading noise and will confidently learn something false.

Give every variant several posts before comparing. If that sounds slow, it is — which is the real
reason most creators never learn anything about their own audience, and keep buying generic advice
instead.

### 5. Throw out variants that stopped being comparable

Sometimes one post gets shared by a large account, lands on an unrelated feed, or catches a trend.
That post is no longer measuring your hook. It is measuring luck.

Exclude it rather than letting it decide the test. A test with three honest variants beats one with
four where the winner is an outlier.

### 6. Write down what you concluded, in usable form

A finished test should leave one plain sentence you can write from:

> "Openings that name the mistake outperform openings that promise a benefit, on this account."

Keep these. After several tests you have a short list of things that are true about *your* audience
— which is exactly the thing no generic advice can give you, and which compounds.

## Reading the result honestly

Three failure modes to watch for in your own reasoning:

**Deciding early.** You will be tempted to stop the moment a variant looks ahead. Ahead after two
posts is not ahead.

**Explaining the winner.** Once you know which won, a story for why appears effortlessly and feels
obvious. Keep the finding, distrust the story — the story is what generalises wrongly into the next
test.

**Testing what does not matter.** If two openings say nearly the same thing in nearly the same way,
the test cannot produce a signal. Make the variants genuinely different.

## A worked example

You want to post about creators underpricing their work.

| Variant | Opening |
|---|---|
| A | "Most creators charge too little." |
| B | "I lost $4,000 last year by underpricing." |
| C | "Your rates are not the problem. Your offer is." |

Same footage, same body, same CTA. Post several of each over two or three weeks. Compare average
view duration. Suppose B and C clearly beat A.

The usable conclusion is not "use numbers". It is closer to: *openings that put something concrete
at stake — a loss, a contradiction — hold this audience; flat assertions do not.* That is a sentence
you can write the next hook from.
